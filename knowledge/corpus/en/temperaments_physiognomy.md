# The Kiyafetname Tradition and the Four Temperaments

## Philosophical Basis

The discipline of inferring character from outward appearance is known in the
Ottoman-Islamic tradition as *ilm al-qiyafa*, *ilm al-sima* or *fann al-firasa*.
Its core argument is that the outward form (*surat*) reflects the inner world
and qualities of the soul (*sirat*) — "the form is a sign of the substance."

The two principal Anatolian texts are the *Kiyafetname* of Hamdullah Hamdi and
the *Marifetname* of Ibrahim Hakki of Erzurum (1757). The *Marifetname* joins
physiognomy to the theory of the four humours.

## The Four Temperaments

The balance of four fluids in the body was held to determine temperament:

1. **Sanguine (blood)** — hot and moist: cheerful, sociable, natural leader,
   optimistic, enterprising.
2. **Choleric (yellow bile)** — hot and dry: strong-willed, decisive, courageous,
   quick to flare, fast to choose.
3. **Melancholic (black bile)** — cold and dry: deep thinker, analytical, given
   to melancholy, artistic, sensitive.
4. **Phlegmatic (phlegm)** — cold and moist: calm, patient, peaceable, steady;
   slow but sure.

Most people are a mixture with one temperament dominant, and the tradition reads
that dominance as a starting disposition rather than a fixed type.

## How the Tradition Reads Character

The classical texts proceed feature by feature — stature, head, forehead, hair,
brows, eyes, nose, mouth, ears, chin — binding each form (large or small, fine
or thick, straight or curved) to a mark of character. A few examples from the
*Marifetname*: middle stature as a sign of balance and good sense; a large head
as maturity and industriousness; coarse hair as courage and soft hair as
pliancy; dark eyes as quickness of understanding; large ears as strong memory.

## A Note on Modern Use

The blunt verdicts found in the classical texts — calling a reader foolish or
treacherous on the evidence of a feature — are artefacts of their historical
context and are not carried over. This tradition is read here as a cultural art
of observation, not as a diagnostic instrument. It yields dispositions and
tendencies, and a tendency is something a person can work with.

What survives as genuinely useful is the temperament framework itself: the four
humours remain a compact and legible vocabulary for describing how someone meets
the world — where their energy comes from, how fast they decide, what depletes
them. That vocabulary is used for insight, never as a personality verdict or a
medical assessment.
