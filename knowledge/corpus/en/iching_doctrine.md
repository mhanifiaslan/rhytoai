# I Ching Doctrine: Change, Trigrams, Lines and Consultation

## The Philosophy of Change

The Book of Changes is not a catalogue of fortunes but a teaching on
timing. The sixty-four hexagrams are sixty-four seasons of life: none is
good or bad in itself; each carries the map of right conduct within its
own weather. Yin and yang do not defeat one another, they give birth to
one another; every condition at its peak is pregnant with its opposite.
So the book's answer is never "what will happen" but "what season are
you inside, and how does one stand well in it". The same question on
another day may bring another hexagram — no contradiction; the river is
the same river, the water is other water.

## The Families of the Eight Trigrams

The eight trigrams read as a family. Qian is the father of heaven:
creative, initiating, enduring. Kun is the mother of earth: receptive,
carrying, nourishing. Zhen is the eldest son — thunder, the first
arousing movement. Kan is the middle son — water and the abyss, master
of danger and depth. Gen is the youngest son — the mountain, the one who
knows how to stop. Xun is the eldest daughter — wind and wood,
penetrating unseen. Li is the middle daughter — fire and light, shining
by clinging. Dui is the youngest daughter — the lake, source of joy and
of speech. A hexagram is the meeting of two trigrams: the lower speaks
of the inner world and the beginning, the upper of the outer world and
the course. Fire over water is ferment; thunder beneath the mountain is
the time of nourishment.

## Judgment, Image and Line: the Three Storeys of a Reading

A cast is read on three storeys. The judgment (tuan) is the verdict on
the whole condition: advance, wait, fortune in small things, do not
cross the great stream. The image (xiang) is the lesson the noble one
draws from the two trigrams' landscape: thunder rolls, and the superior
person sets their life in order. The lines tell your POSITION inside the
condition: in the same hexagram the first line speaks to the one at the
threshold, the fifth to the one at the helm, the sixth to the one about
to overstep. The etiquette of reading descends from whole to position:
first the judgment, then the image, last the moving line. To tear a
line from the whole and read it alone is to rip one street from the map
and call it the city.

## The Doctrine of Moving Lines

Old yang (nine) and old yin (six) are lines at their peak, about to
turn; the weight of the reading rests on their TEXTS. Tradition divides
the emphasis by how many lines move: with no moving line the condition
has settled — only judgment and image are read, and transformation is
not spoken of. With one moving line, the heart of the answer is that
line's word. With two or three, the upper line has the final say; as
moving lines multiply, the judgment of the transformed hexagram comes
forward over the single texts. When all six turn, the special "all
lines" passage of Qian and Kun speaks: transformation itself is the name
of the condition. Legge renders the first line of Qian thus: "the dragon
lying hid (in the deep). It is not the time for active doing" (Legge,
1882) — the moving line always speaks in this register: an image, then a
counsel. The transformed hexagram is not a guarantee of the future but
the direction of the current — a seed is not the fruit's promissory
note.

## The Nuclear Hexagram: the Core of the Situation

Take lines two-three-four as a lower trigram and three-four-five as an
upper one, and the nuclear hexagram (hu gua) appears: the core hidden
inside the visible condition. Within Tai, whose face is peace, ferments
the strain of the marrying maiden (Gui Mei); the core of the completed
Ji Ji is the uncompleted Wei Ji — no ending escapes carrying the seed of
a new beginning. The nuclear hexagram adds to the reading the answer to
"what is brewing at the heart of this" — it does not replace the
judgment, it underlines it.

## The Etiquette of Asking

One approaches the book not as a consumer but as one consulting a wise
friend. The question must be single and real: whoever squeezes two
questions into one cast receives neither answer. Repeating the same
question the same day does not change the answer, it scatters the asker
— tradition says the first cast speaks, and to the importunate the book
closes its secret. One cast a day is the measure of ceremony: clarify
the question within before the coins are thrown, carry the answer for a
day after. The book gives no orders; it does what a mirror does — it
sharpens the asker's own condition.

## Liu Yao: Giving the Lines Stems and Branches

The Wen Wang Gua tradition binds a stem-branch pair to every line
(najia) and seats the hexagram in one of eight palaces. The palace
element becomes the scale: a line of the palace's own element is the
Sibling — peer, rivalry and sharing; the one the palace feeds is the
Offspring — production, remedy and relief; the one that feeds the palace
is the Parent — protection, documents and labour; the one the palace
governs is Wealth — gain, goods and spouse; the one that governs the
palace is the Officer — authority, pressure and trial. The shi (subject)
line stands for the asker, the ying (response) line for the other party;
whatever the question is about, its subject is sought at that line. The
day of the cast adds time to the scale: a line fallen into the void
(kong wang) speaks thin, and a line clashing with the day branch has its
theme shaken that day. Liu Yao adds a ledger to the hexagram's poetry —
read together they give the asker both direction and inventory.

## Limits: Guidance, Not Prophecy

The Book of Changes sells no future. It names no dated events, does not
speak of death or disease, promises no verdicts of gambling or lawsuit.
What it gives is the name of the season and the bearing that suits it;
the rest belongs to the asker's choice. When a cast brings a hexagram
that looks unlucky, that is not a sentence but a weather report: whoever
hears of a storm does not abandon the journey, they change their
preparation. The book's oldest warning still holds: the one who comes
with a true question finds the answer; the one who comes to test the
book clouds their own mirror.
