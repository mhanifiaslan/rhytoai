# BaZi Doctrine: Pillars, Strength, Season and Stars

## The Palaces of the Four Pillars

Each pillar is the palace of an area of life. The year pillar shows the
roots: ancestors, family inheritance, the climate of childhood and the
baggage a person brings into the world. The month pillar carries youth
and the parents, and it is also the career palace — questions of work
look there first. The stem of the day pillar is the person themselves
(the Day Master); the day branch is the spouse palace and describes the
texture of close partnership. The hour pillar shows children, students,
the late years and the trace a person leaves behind.

The pillars also read as a timeline: the year colours the first fifteen
years, the month young adulthood, the day midlife, the hour the late
period. Tension in one palace is not the fate of that area; what matters
is whether the whole chart balances it.

## The Ten Gods: Five Families, Ten Faces

The Ten Gods name the relationship between the Day Master and the other
stems, and they fall into five families. The peers (Bi Jian, Jie Cai)
are companions and rivalry: strength from one's own element, but a share
from the same table. The outputs (Shi Shen, Shang Guan) are expression
and creation: Shi Shen the patient craft, Shang Guan the brilliant
talent impatient with authority. The wealths (Zheng Cai, Pian Cai) are
the return on effort and on opportunity: steady earnings and windfalls.
The authorities (Zheng Guan, Qi Sha) are discipline and rank: Zheng Guan
the lawful governor, Qi Sha a commander when tamed and an oppressor when
loose. The resources (Zheng Yin, Pian Yin) are learning and protection:
Zheng Yin the mother's care and the diploma, Pian Yin intuitive
knowledge picked up at the edges.

The yin and yang face of the same family differ in temper: with the same
polarity the relation runs harder and more direct, with different
polarity it runs smoother and more oblique.

## Hidden Stems: The Stems Inside a Branch

A branch is not a single element; it hides one to three stems. The main
qi is the branch's true voice, the middle and residual qi its whispers.
Inside the Tiger branch, Jia wood leads while Bing fire and Wu earth
stand behind. This is why the verdict "there is no water in this chart"
must not be rushed: the water may be rooted inside a Dragon or a Monkey.
A stem that finds a hidden root of its own element in a branch is called
"rooted" and carries real strength; an unrooted stem, however crowded it
looks, is a leaf in the wind.

## The Strength Verdict: the Month Command Comes First

Whether the Day Master is strong or weak is the verdict that must come
before every other reading of the chart. The heaviest witness is the
month branch: whichever element the season enthrones, the scale is set
from there. An element born in its own season is commanding (wang); the
one the season produces is supported (xiang); the one that produces the
season is resting (xiu); the one that controls the season is restrained
(qiu); the one the season controls is dormant (si). After the month
command come the roots: a root in the day branch is the nearest support,
roots in distant pillars carry less. Among the stems, resources and
peers support; a crowd of outputs, wealth and authority is a burden.

A weak Day Master is not a flaw; what it points to is a life strategy:
leaning on support, on learning, on one's own element. Nor is a strong
Day Master luck by itself; undrained, it turns to flood. Interpretation
begins after the verdict.

## The Useful Element: What Sets the Balance

The useful element (yong shen) is the one that rights the scale of the
chart. In a strong chart one chooses what drains or employs the excess:
output, wealth or authority — preferably one that has a root in the
chart, for no physician prescribes a medicine that is absent. In a weak
chart the resource and the self's element are fed. When the verdict of
balance is uncertain, the prescription must stay cautious: speaking with
certainty where there is none enlarges not balance but error.

## The Climate of the Season: Warmth and Moisture

Before the scale of elements there is a scale of climate. A chart born
in midwinter wants warmth first, whatever the elements say: even if no
fire shows, its direction is fire. The midsummer chart is parched; water
calms and feeds it. In spring and autumn charts the pressure of climate
eases and the scale returns to the elements. For Wood born in winter,
Fire is both warmth and expression; for Fire born in summer, Water is
both brake and depth; for Water born in winter, Fire unfreezes; Earth
born in summer looks to water so as not to crack. The climate adjustment
is where subtlety begins: the same element balance gives a different
prescription in a different season.

## The Stars: Secondary but Talkative Signs

The Shen Sha are symbolic marks that fall upon the pillars, and they are
read after the structure: a star does not establish the verdict, it
adorns or underlines it. The Heavenly Noble (Tian Yi Gui Ren) is the
protective hand that opens doors in hard times; whichever palace it
falls in, help comes from there. The Peach Blossom (Tao Hua) is charm
and social shine; in the palace of relationship it brings movement, in
the palace of career visibility. The Travelling Horse (Yi Ma) is
relocation and change; it shows which area of life is stirring. The
Scholar's Star (Wen Chang) is learning, writing and examination luck.
The Void (Kong Wang) thins the voice of the pillar it falls upon: its
matters run delayed, oblique or inward — not a loss but a call to
deepen. A chart without stars lacks nothing; the structure itself always
speaks first.
